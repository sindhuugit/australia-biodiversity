CONSERVATION PROGRAM 01 / 03 / THE GREAT BARRIER REEF

A Fight for the Reef

Stretching more than 2,300 kilometres along Queensland's coast, the Great Barrier Reef supports extraordinary marine life. But one native predator can become a serious threat when its numbers explode: the coral-eating crown-of-thorns starfish.

Since 2012, the Crown-of-thorns Starfish Control Program has deployed specialist dive teams to survey reefs, detect outbreaks and remove starfish from priority locations. Explore the scale of this work, a documented control case study and historical monitoring patterns.

How to read this choropleth

Each polygon is an official reef marine bioregion. Its colour represents the unweighted mean of available AIMS reef-level modelled median hard-coral-cover estimates for that reporting period. Grey means no matched survey data, not 0% coral.

Important limitations

Surveyed reefs are not a random or complete sample of each bioregion. A coloured polygon does not mean every reef or every point in the region has that coral cover. Site positions and official polygons are matched geographically. Internet access is required for the Reef Authority boundary layer.

Boundaries: © Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority (Marine Bioregions of the Great Barrier Reef — Reef). Coral-cover data: AIMS Long-Term Monitoring Program.

✦ Why this matters: Starfish control targets an important coral predator, while long-term monitoring reveals how coral habitat varies across the Great Barrier Reef. Coral cover is also influenced by marine heatwaves, bleaching, cyclones and other pressures, so the map should not be interpreted as an effect of starfish control alone.
